LightSense:
See the Light Around You with Your Phone

How bright is your desk, really? A reading can give you a starting point. I built a free, offline Android lux meter that uses your phone’s ambient light sensor, with calibration, local records, and multiple languages in one small app.

It started with the lighting at home

I wanted to understand the lighting on my desk, near my monitor, and in different parts of a room. Rather than relying only on how bright things looked, I wanted a tool I could open, read, and save a result with—free to use, with no subscription.

The app is called LightSense · Lux Meter in English and 知照 · 照度计 in Chinese. I started with Android because, on phones equipped with an ambient light sensor, the system can supply readings in lux. That makes it a practical place to develop the measurement workflow first.

It measures illuminance, not a bulb’s lumen output. Lux describes how much light reaches a unit of area at a particular location; lumens describe luminous flux. This app measures illuminance at the phone’s sensor. It cannot directly measure the total light output of a bulb.

The measurement engine reads Android’s Sensor.TYPE_LIGHT; it does not estimate illuminance using the camera. Update frequency, range, and sensor location depend on the phone. Devices without the sensor receive an unsupported-device message.

How to take a reading

  1. Open the measurement screen and locate the phone’s light sensor. It is often near the top, but placement varies. Keep it clear of your hand, case, and other objects.
  2. Place the phone at the location you want to measure. Keep sensor height, orientation, and position consistent when comparing locations.
  3. Hold it still for about three seconds and observe the reading and stability indicator. Reset the statistics when starting a new comparison if needed.
  4. Tap Save record and enter a location and optional notes. Saving pauses measurement; tap Resume measurement for the next reading.
  5. If you have a reference lux meter, measure under conditions that are as similar as possible, then enter its reading to apply single-point proportional calibration.

Applying calibration resets the current statistics and leaves measurement paused. Each saved record retains its calibration information; changing the factor later does not rewrite old records.

Useful readings, with clear limits

A phone is not a professionally calibrated lux meter. Sensor differences, light-source spectra, incident angles, obstructions, and positioning can all affect readings. Single-point calibration can correct some bias, but it cannot remove spectral differences or errors across the full measurement range.

I have checked the response to changing light on a physical Android phone, along with language switching and offline license viewing. The app also passed 16 automated tests. These checks confirm that the features work; they do not certify accuracy against a professional instrument.

Use it for an initial look at home lighting and relative comparisons made with the same device in a consistent position. Engineering acceptance tests and workplace lighting assessments call for professional instruments that meet the relevant requirements.

Local data and bundled license notices

The current version requests no internet, camera, or location permissions. It has no login, advertising, or subscription. Measurement summaries and calibration profiles stay on the device. Clearing app data or uninstalling deletes them.

The app uses third-party components including AndroidX and Kotlin. Full license texts and required copyright notices are bundled with the APK for offline reading. Using those components does not mean this project’s source code is public; its source repository remains private.

Future ideas include a better measurement experience, multi-point measurements, and CSV export. These are possible next steps, not features included in version 0.2.1.
